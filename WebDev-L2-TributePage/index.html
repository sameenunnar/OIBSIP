A Tribute To

Ada Lovelace

The mathematician who saw a future in machines before anyone had built one worth imagining.

Her Life

Augusta Ada King, Countess of Lovelace, was born in London in 1815, the only legitimate child of the poet Lord Byron. Her mother, wary of the "poetic" temperament she associated with Byron, steered Ada firmly toward mathematics and logic from an early age — a decision that would shape the course of computing history a century later.

As a young woman, Ada was introduced to Charles Babbage, the inventor of the Difference Engine and, later, the far more ambitious Analytical Engine. Where most of Babbage's contemporaries saw an elaborate calculating machine, Ada saw something else entirely: a device that could, in principle, manipulate any symbol according to rules, not just numbers.

In 1843, while translating an Italian mathematician's paper on the Analytical Engine, Ada added a set of her own notes — nearly three times the length of the original text. Tucked inside was a step-by-step method for the machine to compute Bernoulli numbers, a sequence that could not simply be looked up or calculated by hand. Many historians consider this the first published algorithm intended for execution by a machine, which is why Ada is often credited as the world's first computer programmer.

She also wrote, with striking prescience, that such a machine might one day compose music or produce graphics if the right rules were given to it — an idea that would not be taken seriously again for well over a hundred years. Ada Lovelace died in 1852 at the age of 36, long before any machine capable of running her algorithm was ever built.

Key Milestones

  1. 1815

    Born in London

    Raised largely by her mother, Lady Byron, who insisted on a rigorous education in mathematics and science.

  2. 1833

    Meets Charles Babbage

    Introduced to Babbage's Difference Engine at a demonstration, sparking a lifelong intellectual partnership.

  3. 1843

    Publishes the first algorithm

    Her notes on the Analytical Engine include a method for computing Bernoulli numbers — widely regarded as the first algorithm written for a machine.

  4. 1843

    Imagines general-purpose computing

    Suggests the Analytical Engine could manipulate more than numbers — including music and graphics — decades before such ideas were revisited.

  5. 1980

    Honoured by name

    The U.S. Department of Defense names its newly standardised programming language "Ada" in her honour.

"That brain of mine is something more than merely mortal, as time will show."

— Ada Lovelace, in a letter, 1843
